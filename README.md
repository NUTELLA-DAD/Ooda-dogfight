# OODA Dogfight

An open-world WW2 dogfighting game in Unity: arcade-paced like a space-shooter, but free-roaming instead of on rails. The flight model is the OODA tool's point-mass model (`dads-debreif`, `src/core/point-mass.js`), ported to C#.

## Milestone 1: fly the P-51 (now the sandbox)

- A P-51D with a spinning propeller, a clear canopy, and the tiger pilot seated inside (Humanoid rig) who looks into the turn.
- The OODA flight model: G and roll from the stick, energy from thrust minus drag, the stall line and the G limits, smoothed G onset and roll. No assists: let go in a bank and it stays banked.
- Chase and cockpit cameras, a tuning HUD, and a test island with sea, hills, clouds and towers.

## The game: turn-based dogfight (Mustang against Spitfire)

Each turn lasts 3 seconds, and both pilots plan at the same time. You drag a flight-path arrow to say where the nose should point when the turn ends, inside a white ring that shows how far the aircraft can turn in 3 s. Past it a band shades from white into red: in it you overpull: the stick goes beyond the stall line, the turn mushes, and held long enough it departs into a spin. The arrow turns red and the screen says what will happen; the next turn's arrow flies the spin recovery. The aircraft flies the best roll and pull to get there, with the same flight model as free flight. A holographic copy of your aircraft shows where the turn leaves you, at the end of the blue ghost path, a shaded band that tilts with your bank. Orange tracers show where your rounds will go: they arc and take time to arrive, so you must lead the target. A tracer turns red when it hits (if the enemy holds) and yellow when it passes close; a red band on the burst timeline marks the hit window, the moments when rounds fired would connect. The readout shows HITS in red, and how many of four enemy moves (hold, break left, break right, pull up) the burst still hits; the target's ghost glows red. The screen warns, and the path turns red, if the turn flies you into the ground or an obstacle. A red line shows the Spitfire if it keeps doing what it's doing. Then press **Fly turn**: the AI plans without seeing your plan, both aircraft fly, and the turn plays back. Shoot it down to win; get shot down or hit the ground and you lose.

The aircraft turn as the real ones did. The G you can pull is capped by the stall line (slow means mushy) and by the G limit, so each type has a corner speed where it turns quickest and tightest; pulling hard bleeds speed through induced drag, and what thrust is left decides the turn you can hold. Each fighter is fitted to wartime and test figures at sea level with WEP: the Spitfire IX circles in about 18.5 s, the Bf 109 G-6 in 20.5 s, the P-51D and the P-38L in 22 s, the A6M2 Zero in 16 s, the F-4 holds about 14 deg/s, the F-15 about 19 and the Su-27 about 21; top speeds and climbs are fitted too (`Tools~/FlightCoreCheck` prints the table). The AI flies to its type's strengths against yours: it keeps its own corner speed, turns when it out-turns you, and goes up and down (speed and height in hand) when it doesn't. Its ghosts are spread across different kinds of move (a climb, a dive, a turn or hard turn each way, straight on), each with the real chance it flies that kind; the unlikely ones are faint and may read under 1%.

Build it with **OODA > Build Turn Battle** (after Build Test World), open `Assets/OODA/Scenes/TurnBattle.unity`, and press Play. The free-flight `TestWorld` stays as the sandbox.

### The gun funnel

Hover over the burst bar while planning and the chase camera eases round to sit straight behind your ghost at that moment of the turn, lined up with its guns. A gun cross marks the middle and the gun funnel shows your rounds in the air then (as if the trigger were held all turn) as they look from the seat: two thick green lines a wingspan apart, wide close in and narrowing with range, bending away from your turn, with a rung every 1,000 ft and a horizon line. The enemy's likely moves show as red ghost models where each would be at that moment, the likeliest solid and the others fainter by their chance, each with its %; one turns bright red with ON TARGET when your rounds are on it then. Fit its wings across the funnel and set your burst there (F still fires at the likeliest move's hits). Your blue ghost sits low in front of you. The view holds a moment after the mouse leaves the bar, then eases back; there is no funnel in the normal chase view. **K** steps the funnel through hover (the default), lined up all the time, and off. The old lead ring on the target's path is now a toggle, **L**, off at first. It is all in `TurnBattle.Funnel.cs`.

With only one fighter of yours, it is always yours to fly: it starts each turn on your control (straight ahead), never on its AI pilot's plan.

### Training: the dogfight tutorial

**Training** on the title screen (**T**) teaches a new player how a dogfight works, one small goal at a time. Each lesson is a one on one, your P-51 against a Bf 109 over the farmland, with a coach panel on the left that says what to do now and ticks each goal off when you do it (checked between turns, never mid-playback):

1. **Plan a turn**: steer with the stick, see the blue path and ghost, fly the turn.
2. **Turn hard, not too hard**: pull into the stick shaker, then fly a hard turn out of the red (overpull).
3. **Speed is life**: the throttle starts at 60%: full throttle and WEP, then two hard turns watching your speed.
4. **Get on its six**: an unarmed Bf 109 that manoeuvres; read its ghosts and get behind it (within 3,000 ft, it within 35° of your nose, you within 60° of its tail).
5. **Guns: lead the target**: a throttled-back target drone; plan a burst that hits (the red band, or F), hit it, shoot it down.
6. **Defend: the break turn**: a Bf 109 on your tail with softened guns; break hard or use an extra (speed brake, rocket, balloon mine), and survive four turns.
7. **Final: one on one**: head-on against a Bf 109 that fights for real (guns and airframe a little softened).

The drone in lessons 1, 2, 3 and 5 has no pilot and no guns: it holds its course, so the preview shows exactly where it goes. Nobody but you gets missiles or the arcade specials. **Next lesson** (Page Down) and **Back** (Page Up) move between lessons, **Retry** (or R once the fight is over) flies one again, **Hide** folds the coach away. `-tutorial N` on the command line opens straight into lesson N. The lessons, their setups and the goal checks are plain C# in `Turn/Core/Tutorial.cs`; the coach on screen is `Turn/TurnBattle.Tutorial.cs`. `Tools~/TutorialCheck` (`dotnet run`) checks that every lesson builds and arms the foe as it says, that each goal ticks when it should and not before, that the drone holds its course, and that the lessons can be finished: a student in your seat trying arrows against the bright ghost (as a player does) gets on the 109's six, shoots the drone down, and lives through the break turn lesson.

### Dogfight: up to 4 v 4, the side controls, the rocket and the speed brake

Pick **Dogfight** on the title screen and press Play: the **squadron briefing** opens, a 3D airfield at the south end of the town's runway (`UI/BriefingStage.cs`). Your flight is parked on the left and the bandits on the right, lead nearest the middle, each plane on its wheels with its pilot standing in front of it (the seated pilot posed standing through its Humanoid muscles), and a gold ring round the one picked. Click a pilot or a name plate, or use the left and right arrows, to pick one; **Change Pilot** (P, pad X; right-click or Shift goes back) steps him through the type's usual pilot, the tiger, the wolf, the wizard, the skeleton, the bear, the fox, the koi, the lion, the penguin and the spectre (the F-4 keeps its crew), and **Change Aircraft** (C, pad Y) steps his plane through every type: P-51D Mustang, Spitfire IX, Bf 109 G-6, F-4 Phantom, P-38L Lightning, A6M2 Zero, F-15C Eagle or Su-27 Flanker, any type on either side. The - and + under each banner (or - and +, the bumpers, for the picked pilot's side) set how many planes each side has, 1 to 4. **Mission** (M, pad Select) opens the start: Random (a new one each time you fly again), Head-on, Side by side, On their six, Them on your six, Crossing (them from your right at right angles), High perch (you 2,500 ft above and behind them, out to one side) or Bounced (the other way round); the range (close, medium, far: 0.6, 1 and 1.7 times the usual gaps), the height (low, about 400 m over the basin; medium; high, 4,400 ft higher) and the speed (220, 300 or 380 KIAS), with a map of where everyone starts, from above. **Ready** (Enter, pad A) flies; Back (Esc, pad B) closes the Mission panel or goes back to the title. What you pick is kept for next time (`UI/DogfightSetupScreen.cs`; the setup itself is `Turn/Core/DogfightSetup.cs`, which `Scenarios.Dogfight` builds the battle from). **OODA > Build Title** gives the title the planes to park (the turn battle's prefabs, `Prefabs/<type>.prefab` for a new type). On the title, the up and down arrows still change your side's size and Page Up and Page Down theirs, and Q starts a quick 1 v 1, P-51 against a Bf 109.

The **P-38L Lightning** is the Hi3D twin-engine model (`Art/P38`), cut from 300k triangles to about 125k by `Tools~/ArtPipeline/p38.py`, which also splits off its canopy and its two propellers (each spinning about its own hub, outward at the top) and places the seat; `build_cockpit.py P38` builds its cockpit, and the tiger flies it (any of the pilots can). Its numbers are estimates from public figures: two turbocharged Allisons, so it is a little slower than the Mustang low down and the fastest of the piston fighters above 20,000 ft, it climbs well but rolls and turns heavily; its 20 mm and four .50s all sit in the nose, so its cone is tight at any range, and two engines make it a little tougher (and a bigger target). `OODA > Build Turn Battle` makes `Prefabs/P38.prefab`.

The **A6M2 Zero** is the Hi3D cartoon Zero (`Art/Zero`), scaled to the real 12 m span and cut from 300k triangles to about 100k by `Tools~/ArtPipeline/zero.py` (after `glb_prep.py`, which reads Hi3D's GLBs), which also splits off its greenhouse canopy and its three-blade propeller and places the seat; `build_cockpit.py Zero` builds its cockpit and `glb_tex.py` makes the 4K base colour and the metallic-smoothness map. The fox flies it, with the koi and the others on the pilot button. Its numbers are estimates from public figures, fitted to a 16 s circle, 290 mph low down, 331 mph at 15,000 ft and 3,100 ft/min: it out-turns everything and corners slowest, but it has no WEP, everything else out-runs and out-dives it, its roll is capped low (the ailerons stiffen with speed), and with no armour or self-sealing tanks it goes down after fewer hits; two slow 20 mm cannon and two 7.7 mm guns hit hard but scatter. `OODA > Build Turn Battle` makes `Prefabs/Zero.prefab`.

The **F-15C Eagle** is the Hi3D cartoon Eagle (`Art/F15`), scaled to the real 13.05 m span and cut from 2 million faces to about 120k by `Tools~/ArtPipeline/f15.py`, which opens its two-seat bubble whole (like the Zero's greenhouse: the pilot under its front half, a deck behind him), places the seat, the two nozzle exits (`NozzleL`, `NozzleR`) and four missile stations under the wings (`MissileMount_L1`, `_R1`, `_L2`, `_R2`); `build_cockpit.py F15` builds a jet-grey cockpit and `f15_tex.py` cleans the 4K base colour and tones the chrome down to paint. The tiger flies it and, unlike the F-4, any of the pilots can. Its numbers are estimates from public figures on the F-4's jet model: +9 G, about 19 deg/s sustained low down in afterburner (a 19 s circle, against the F-4's 26 s) and 27 deg/s at a 354 KIAS corner, near 650 kt dry and 760 kt (Mach 1.15) in afterburner at sea level, about Mach 2.35 at 36,000 ft, a 54,000 ft/min climb and a 250 deg/s roll. Its M61A1 fires for about 9 s and it carries 4 missiles and 8 flares; Space lights the afterburner (BURNER), with flames from its own nozzles. `OODA > Build Turn Battle` makes `Prefabs/F15.prefab`.

The **koi** flies in a **fishbowl helmet**: a glass bowl round its head, filled with water nearly to the top (just over its dorsal fin), three bubbles on its left and a riveted brass collar at the neck. `Editor/KoiFishbowl.cs` builds it (called from `PilotCast` wherever the koi is seated, so **OODA > Build Turn Battle** and **Build Title** put it on), skinned to the head bone: it turns with the head look, shrinks away with the head in the cockpit view and goes with the pilot when he bails out. The glass and water cast no shadow, so the face stays lit. `Tools~/ArtPipeline/koi_fishbowl_preview.py` builds the same shapes in Blender and seats the koi in each fighter for renders.

The **Su-27 Flanker** is Kenny's Hi3D cartoon Flanker (`Art/Su27`), in its blue sea camouflage, scaled to the real 14.7 m span over the wingtip rails and cut from 300k triangles to about 120k by `Tools~/ArtPipeline/su27.py`, which opens its bubble whole (the pilot under its front half, the spine's hump behind him), places the seat, the two nozzle exits (`NozzleL`, `NozzleR`) and six missile stations under the wings (`MissileMount_L1` to `_R3`, inboard first); `build_cockpit.py Su27` builds its jet-grey cockpit and `su27_tex.py` cleans the 4K base colour. The bear flies it, and any of the pilots can. Its numbers are estimates from public figures on the F-15's jet model: two AL-31Fs, so a little more thrust for its weight than the Eagle (1.14:1 lit), a low stall and a high alpha from its lifting body, +9 G: about 21 deg/s sustained low down in afterburner (a 17 s circle, past the F-15's 19) and 29.5 deg/s at a 330 KIAS corner, about 650 kt dry and 753 kt (Mach 1.14) lit at sea level, Mach 2.35 at 36,000 ft, a 58,000 ft/min climb and a 270 deg/s roll. Its one GSh-30-1 30 mm hits much harder than the M61 and holds a tight cone, but it fires for only about 5.5 s; it carries 6 heat-seekers (R-73s) on the wings, 2 radar missiles (R-27s) for its N001 radar (33,000 ft), 8 flares and 6 chaff; Space lights the afterburner (BURNER), with flames from its own nozzles. `OODA > Build Turn Battle` makes `Prefabs/Su27.prefab`.

Against the AI your guns get the forgiving hit radius whatever you fly (the P-51's numbers always had it); an enemy P-51 doesn't. In the fight the F-4 has its M61 cannon (about 7 s of fire, hard hitting) and 4 missiles, and its WEP box is its afterburner (BURNER), with the flame in playback. It has no pilot yet. For a game over the network the host fixes a random start with `Resolved` and sends `ToBytes()`: the same bytes build the same battle on both machines (`Tools~/NetCheck` checks it). The bot takes `-dogfightcraft Spitfire,Phantom Bf109,Mustang` for the aircraft on each side, and `-setupshots <folder>` on the built game's command line saves three pictures of the briefing (2 v 2, 4 v 3, and the Mission panel) and quits. You fly every one of yours: **Tab** (Shift+Tab back, the roster buttons along the top, or the pad's View button) moves you to your next plane, which becomes the one the stick, the ghost and the lead ring work on. A plane you visit in a turn flies the plan you leave it with, starting from what its AI pilot would do; one you never visit flies itself. Each turn starts back on your lead. When the plane you are in goes down you carry on in another; the fight is lost only when your whole flight is. **N** picks which enemy you are after (red brackets mark it). The roster along the top is grouped by flight: a button for each of your flights (Blue, Red, Bombers) with how many are still up, and the planes of the one you're flying folded out after it; click another flight's button to fold out its planes instead. Under the roster, one bar shows the flight you're in, a small picture of its formation, its order and its shape; click it to fold out the orders (Rejoin, Auto Fight, You Control) and, when you lead the flight, the formations, each a tile with a picture of the shape from above. Under the hood, switching planes is `Battle.Swap(0, k)`: aircraft k becomes aircraft 0 everywhere, taking its route, slot and runway place with it (`TurnBattle.Flight.cs`).

The arrow and its yellow ball are gone. The bottom of the screen is a cockpit (`TurnBattle.Cockpit.cs`): a gunmetal instrument panel shaped as a trapezoid, its padded coaming flat across the middle, over the top of the stick so it stands inside it, and sloping down to the bottom corners, with the instruments grouped left of the stick: the airspeed indicator and the altimeter side by side, and under them the G meter (red past the airframe's limit) and the attitude indicator (a sky and ground ball that banks and pitches, a rung each 10 degrees); the system lights (ENG, FUEL, CTRL, WING; `SystemLight` says what each shows) run in a row along the top of the dash over the stick; while planning they read where the turn starts, in playback they follow the plane. Laid out in it: the **stick in the middle** standing between the **rudder pedals** (Kenny's Hi3D model, `Resources/Pedals.obj`, drawn by the gauge's own camera; the bar swings with the rudder, the right pedal away for right rudder; press or drag on them to set it; without the model the old pedal bar comes back), the **throttle quadrant on the left** (the lever, which you drag toward the stick to open, and beside it the throttle slider and the rocket and speed brake switches; a jet has a burner switch too), and the **gun MFD right of the stick**, set in the panel (`TurnBattle.Mfd.cs`): a green multifunction display in its bezel. Its glass shows the gun funnel from your seat at the trigger bar's moment (your rounds as two lines a wingspan apart, a rung at 1,000 and 2,000 ft, the gun cross, the horizon and the target's wings in red), the hits and the missile's state along the top, and the trigger bar along the bottom (drag on it to set the burst, as before). Its push buttons: FIRE at the hits, the missile's ARM, the timeline and IR along the bottom; the funnel's mode, the lead ring and the next target down the left. Flare hold and the arcade specials are a row of switches in the bottom right corner past it (over it when the window is too narrow). The buttons are drawn as cockpit switches (`TurnBattle.Switches.cs`): a plate with a lamp that lights when on and a toggle bat that flips up. There is no WEP switch on a piston fighter: the throttle's last notch is WEP, so full throttle (Space pushes it to the stop) flies on war emergency power; the jet keeps its afterburner on Space. Drag the stick to steer (down pulls, sideways rolls into it, past the shaker into the overpull band). WASD and the pad move it as before. **F2** brings the arrow back as a dev view, and it comes back by itself when the window is too narrow for the stick. There is no shaker or overpull caption: the stick itself shakes, hard near your tightest turn and violently in the overpull band, and a gamepad rumbles with it. The OVERPULL, SPINNING, STALL and DEPARTED warnings are a dev view (**F3**); the shaker and the ghost path tell you. The layout is `TurnBattle.LayoutControls`.

**Rocket** (1, the d-pad right, or the button in the planning panel): lit at the start of a turn, it shoves for 2.5 s at 1.3 times the weight (about 60 kt more), then needs 30 s, ten normal turns, before it is ready again. **Speed brake** (2, the d-pad left): out for the turn, 1.0 G of drag at 300 KIAS (1.4 G on a jet), growing with the square of the speed, so it sheds about 55 kt a turn when fast and much less when slow. The AI pilots use both: the rocket when it leaves them clearly better placed (or whenever they're slow), the brake with the throttle chopped to make you overshoot. The numbers are `rocketG`, `rocketSec`, `rocketCooldownSec` and `brakeG` in `AircraftNumbers`. A flame shows behind a plane on its rocket during playback, and grey panels when its brake is out.

### IR view and flares

**I** (any time, or the IR button over the planning panel) turns on the IR view: the screen as a heat-seeker's thermal camera sees it, the world a dim grey with the sky cold and dark (`Fx/IrView.shader`, which reads the camera's opaque texture, through `Resources/IrView.mat`), and every heat source a white-hot glow as bright as the seeker sees it from where the camera is. It uses the same numbers the missile locks with (`Heat` in `Turn/Core/Combat.cs`): each engine by its throttle (`heatIdle`, `heatFull` in `CombatNumbers`), WEP or the afterburner (`heatWep`: the F-4's burner more than doubles it) and the rocket (+2), all of it from astern but only a quarter from ahead; a missile's motor; and the flares, the hottest things in the sky. The seeker's lock range grows with the square root of the heat it sees: 9,000 ft on a piston fighter at full throttle from behind, about 6,400 ft at idle, up to 13,500 ft on a jet in afterburner or a plane on its rocket. The views and the replay show it too.

**Flares**: every fighter carries 6 (the F-4 8, the B-17 none). They go by themselves: when a heat-seeker tracking you closes inside 3,000 ft, a pair goes out behind you, 0.35 s apart, and each has one chance to pull the seeker off onto itself: 0.9 x 1.6 / (1.6 + the heat it sees on you). A pair saves a piston fighter at full throttle about 80% of the time, a jet in afterburner about 35%: throttle back and stay out of the burner when one is coming. **3** (or the Flares button) holds them for the turn. A red MISSILE ON YOU warning says when one is tracking you. The AI pilots let theirs go the same way. A decoyed missile chases the flare until it burns out (3.5 s), then flies on unguided. `Tools~/HeatCheck` (`dotnet run`) checks the lock ranges, the flares' odds, and that flares survive a clone, a subset and a snapshot.

### Radar and radar missiles (jets only)

The F-4, the F-15 and the Su-27 carry a radar and two radar missiles (Sparrows; R-27s on the Su-27) besides their heat-seekers, and 6 bundles of chaff; the warbirds have none, so their fights stay gun fights. The rules are `Radar` in `Turn/Core/Combat.cs` and the radar's bookkeeping in `Battle` (`TrackRadars`, `Locked`, `Illuminating`, `DropChaff`); the screen side is `Turn/TurnBattle.Radar.cs`.

- **The radar** sees every enemy that isn't stealthy inside a 60 degree cone off the nose, out to 30,000 ft (the F-15's 36,000, the Su-27's 33,000). It tracks one: the target you're after (N) when it's in the cone, else the one it was tracking, else the one nearest the nose. Keep the same one in the cone for a whole turn (3 s) and it is **locked**. A jet's MFD has a radar page (the RDR push button on its left, or **U**; it shows by itself while the target is beyond 6,000 ft): a B-scope with the cone across and the range up, a blip for each enemy with a tick for its heading, a box round the one tracked (red once locked), your radar missiles in flight, and the lock filling along the bottom. Its top line says when the planned turn will finish the lock.
- **The radar missile**: arm it with **J** (or the RDR push button on the radar page) and it leaves the first moment your radar is locked, 1,500 ft or more away. It burns longer than a heat-seeker and reaches much farther, but it guides only while your radar keeps that target in its cone (out to 1.25 times its range): fire, and you are committed to pointing at them. Turn them out of your cone, or switch the radar to someone else, and it goes dumb.
- **The notch** beats it: fly square across the missile's line of sight, within 25 degrees of 90, for 1 s, and with no closing speed to see the missile loses you for good. When a radar missile is on you the planning view shows a green notch bar across your ghost (square to the missile's line of sight, the band's edges either side, a faint red line to the missile), your path turns green where it is in the notch, and a line at the top says how long the turn holds it and when the missile loses you.
- **Chaff** goes by itself, like the flares: a pair of bundles at a radar missile closing inside 4,000 ft, 0.35 s apart, each with a 20% chance to break it, 70% while you are in the notch. **3** holds flares and chaff together (the switch reads CM HOLD on a jet). Flares never go at a radar missile.
- **Warnings**: RADAR SPIKE when an enemy radar is tracking you (with how long until it locks), RADAR LOCK once it has, and RADAR MISSILE with its range once one is guiding on you.
- **The AI** pilots in jets track their target, fire when locked, and fly the notch when one is on them (a radar missile still guiding on them at the end of a try costs it).

`Tools~/RadarCheck` (`dotnet run`) checks the lock (a whole turn, not a warbird, not through stealth, starting over on a new target), that the missile hits a target running away and one 45 degrees off, that the notch and the shooter turning away both break it, chaff's odds in and out of the notch, that radar state survives a clone, a subset, a swap and a snapshot, and that the AI fires and notches. Diagram: `notch_diagram.png` in the project files.

### The loadout: two picks each

With **Arcade** on, each pilot brings **two** capabilities to the fight, picked in the squadron briefing: **Loadout** on the foot bar (**L**, the pad's right stick press), or click the picks on a name plate. The panel shows the picked pilot (the arrows step through them, bandits too) and a tile for each capability with what it does; a click or its number key (1 to 9, then 0) adds it in place of the older pick. **Same for your whole flight** copies the two to the rest of the side. The name plates show each plane's two (MSL + MINE). Everyone always carries his guns, his flares (a jet its chaff and radar too) and the speed brake. The picks:

| Pick | What it brings |
|---|---|
| Heat-seekers | the type's IR missiles (2, a jet 4) |
| Rocket | the rocket (piston fighters only: a jet's afterburner does that job) |
| Balloon mines | 2 mines |
| Heat blade | 1 charge |
| Stealth | 1 charge |
| Jammer | 1 charge |
| Radar missiles | the jet's Sparrows (jets only: a warbird has no radar) |
| Ice | the plane cased in ice all fight, covered in icicles: no heat-seeker can lock it, and one already on its way shatters on it harmlessly; it is as cold as the sky in the IR view. Radar missiles and guns still bite |
| Gravity well | 2 gravity wells, thrown ahead and into your turn: they pull planes, missiles and rounds in (see below) |
| Portals | 2 portal pairs: fly into one ring and come out of the other, where you put it (see below) |

A plane starts with heat-seekers and mines (a jet with both its missiles); a plane added brings what the one before it brings; a jet that had the rocket (or a warbird radar missiles) swaps it for something it can carry. The AI pilots fly with what they were given and use only that. The loadouts go to your friend with the rest of the setup; with Arcade off it is the straight dogfight (heat-seekers and the rocket, a jet its radar missiles, no specials), and the tutorial keeps its full kit. The rules are `Turn/Core/Loadout.cs` (`Kit` for the list: new ones go on the end, with what they put on the plane in `Loadout.Apply`), the panel `UI/DogfightSetupScreen.cs`, the ice's look `Turn/IceCoat.cs` (a frost over every part, and icicles hanging from the lowest points of the airframe; it reads the model's mesh, so the aircraft models are imported with Read/Write on). `Tools~/SpecialsCheck` checks the picks, the swaps, the trip to bytes, and the ice.

### Arcade specials: the balloon mine, the heat blade and stealth

With **Arcade** on (the switch under the VS badge in the squadron briefing, or **G**; on by default, and sent to your friend with the rest of the setup), the fighters in a dogfight carry the specials their loadouts picked (see above) (a game liberty, like the missiles); with it off, and in the other missions, there are none and their buttons are hidden. They are planned like the rocket for the start of a turn, with the buttons over the IR and flare buttons or the keys. The rules are `Turn/Core/Specials.cs`, the look `Turn/TurnBattle.Specials.cs`; the counts are `balloonMines`, `heatBlades` and `stealths` in `CombatNumbers`, which `Specials.Arm` fills when `DogfightSetup.arcade` is on.

- **Balloon mine** (4; 2 each): Kenny's red balloon with a spiked mine hanging under it (`Resources/Mine`, his Hi3D model cut from 300k to 20k triangles and its paint's baked blotches smoothed by `Tools~/ArtPipeline/mine.py` and `mine_tex.py`), let go 50 ft behind you at any moment of the 3 s turn: a red marker on the MFD's trigger bar says when (drag it; **4** steps it through 0, 1, 2 and 3 s, then off; the MINE switch turns it on and off), and a red ring on your path shows where it goes. It slows to a hang in about a second, then drifts slowly on (about 12 ft/s the way you were flying, swaying and bobbing a little), arms after 0.6 s (its light starts blinking), and goes off on the first enemy that flies within 70 ft of it, with a blast that wears every system of a fighter most of the way to out (see Systems damage). It hangs there for 15 s, so a tailer who planned to follow you straight flies into it, and anyone can blunder into it later. Your own side flies through yours unharmed. While planning each mine shows with a faint red ring for its fuse, and the one you're about to drop shows where it ends the turn.
- **Heat blade** (5; 1 each): the whole plane glows red hot for the turn (a pulsing red copy of every part of the airframe laid over it, `TurnAircraftView.SetRedHot`) and its guns stay cold. Pass within 45 ft of an enemy and it is cut in half (down at once, a kill for you) while you fly on; two blades meeting both go down, and a friendly collision is still a collision. It adds 4 to your heat from every side, so every heat-seeker locks you from farther (up to 13,500 ft) and flares hardly help: it lights you up in the IR view.
- **Jammer** (7; 1 each): every enemy within 9,000 ft is jammed for the next two planning phases. A jammed AI pilot plans against a scrambled picture: everyone on your side moved up to 900 ft and turned up to 70 degrees (`Jam.Scramble`, the same dice on every machine), so it aims and breaks the wrong way. Jammed yourself, the enemies' paths and ghosts jump to the wrong place and flicker, their predicted moves, the funnel and their projected tracers are gone, and a purple JAMMED warning shows: go by the planes themselves.
- **Gravity well** (8; 2 with the loadout pick): a violet whirlpool you throw ahead and into your turn at any moment of the 3 s turn: a violet marker on the MFD's trigger bar says when (drag it; **8** steps it through 0, 1, 2 and 3 s, then off; the WELL switch turns it on and off). It settles 600 ft ahead of you and 700 ft toward where your plan points the nose (with no turn planned, toward your lift: above you when wings level), slowing to a hang in a second or so. It opens 0.4 s after the throw and pulls on everything near it for 6 s (two turns), fading over the last second: every plane, both sides and you too, at up to 2.5 G; a missile at up to 22 G; and a packet of rounds at up to 60 G (they're light). The pull grows to its strongest at the core's edge, 700 ft out, and falls with the square of the range past it, to nothing at 3,500 ft. Three uses: thrown into a hard turn it adds to your wing's pull, about a quarter to a half more turn over two turns (`SpecialsCheck` measures it at 220, 300 and 380 kt); a tailer's stream bends off its line as it passes (a 109 shooting from behind lands about a quarter of its hits); and a missile drawn within 450 ft of its middle loses its target for good and flies on unguided, so throw it ahead with a heat-seeker on your tail and fly through: the missile is dragged in after you. It does no damage and is no obstacle. While planning, the open wells show as whirlpools with their core ring, and the one you're about to throw shows its path and where it settles. The rules are `Well` and `Specials` in `Turn/Core/Specials.cs` and the pull in `Battle` (`WellPull`); the plane is pushed by `FlightModel.Push`, free fall the pilot doesn't feel. The look and the switch are `Turn/TurnBattle.Well.cs`.
- **Stealth** (6; 1 each): two normal turns (6 s) unseen. The other side's AI pilots, flak and B-17 gunners don't see you. A stealthy enemy vanishes from your screen with its path, ghosts, projection, funnel and target bracket; only its heat still shows in the IR view, and **a heat-seeker still locks it**. Yours disappears too, leaving only a dotted cyan outline of it (marching dashes round its shape from above and its fin) so you can still see where you are; in the replay every stealthy plane shows that way. Firing your guns or a missile shows you again at once.

- **Portals** (2 pairs with the loadout pick): a linked pair of riveted brass rings, the entry's rim green and the exit's magenta, each a window onto what lies past the other (a second camera at the other ring sees what you would see flying through, `Turn/PortalRings.cs` and `Fx/PortalView.shader`). The entry opens round you on your path at the moment you pick on the MFD's trigger bar (drag its green marker; **9** steps it through 0, 1, 2 and 3 s, then off; the PORTAL switch turns it on and off), so you go through then. The exit is yours to place: turned on, it starts 1,400 ft behind the enemy you're after (N), facing his way, and the next click in the view puts it there instead, at your height (the mouse wheel raises or lowers it while you place it, and a ring shows how far it can go, 15,000 ft); drag from the click to point it. **Shift+9** or the EXIT switch places it again. It is kept 300 ft over the ground and at least 2,000 ft from the entry. Anything that flies through either ring comes out of the other, keeping its speed, and its heading and bank as they were to the ring it went into: planes of both sides (a tailer who follows you in comes out behind you), rounds (a stream fired into one comes out of the other) and missiles (one on your tail follows you through; one that doesn't loses you). Both ways, for 6 s (two turns), then it closes. The ghost path and the tracers show the jump; in playback the camera swings round with you as you come out. The rules are `Portal` and `Specials` in `Turn/Core/Specials.cs` and the crossings in `Battle` (`WarpPlanes`, `WarpBullet`); the switch, the marker and the placing are `Turn/TurnBattle.Portal.cs`. `-portalshots` (with `-dogfight`) on the built game's command line saves six pictures of it beside the exe and quits.

The AI pilots use them: a gravity well when a missile is on them, a mine when someone is on their tail with guns on them (a gravity well when they're out of mines, then a portal out onto the tailer's six), a portal onto their target's six when it is out past 5,000 ft and not closing, the jammer when they're out of both, stealth when they are hurt and out of both (and not firing), and the blade when one of their tries would fly through their target. `Tools~/SpecialsCheck` (`dotnet run`) checks each of them against the battle core, the AI using them, and that they survive a clone, a subset, a swap and a snapshot.

### Rudder pedals, tail slides and hammerheads

There is no stunt button: these come out of the flight model. The **rudder pedals** are the bar under the stick (drag it, or hold **Z**/**C**; **X** centres them with the stick). At speed the rudder only skids the nose a little. Pull straight up and let the speed bleed off: below about 120 ft/s with the nose over 50 degrees up the wing has nothing left and the plane flies on its prop and its tail (`FlightModel.StepVertical`). Then:

- **Tail slide**: hold the stick and pedals centred. The plane stops, slides backwards, and the tail weathervanes the nose over and down; at 150 ft/s with the nose along the flight path it flies again.
- **Hammerhead**: kick a pedal near the top. The prop blast over the rudder swings the nose sideways over the top and straight down, with no slide back. The power matters: a prop's slipstream gives the rudder bite even at a standstill, a jet's doesn't (`propWash`), so the Phantom swings over slower.
- **Barrel roll**: not its own manoeuvre yet. The nearest today is bank points on the timeline that keep rolling while you pull; this isn't checked headless.

`Tools~/VerticalCheck` (`dotnet run`) flies each of them through the battle core.

### The island

The island is 20 km across in three zones. The north is snowy peaks: about 70 copies of the snowy mountain model (`Art/Mountain`), each turned, stretched and sometimes mirrored and overlapping into massifs, with canyons between them. The middle is pine valleys and the chalet lake. The south is farmland (a patchwork of ripe wheat, young green grain, pasture and meadow with hedgerows; the crop fields are drawn as rows of grain with bare furrows and tramlines, along or across each field, all in the ground texture so they cost nothing extra to draw), the factory town and the coast. Copies of the grass hill model (`Art/Foothill`) are the foothills and the farmland's swells. The ground under each model is raised to just under it, so the ground check, the AI, the clouds and the trees all treat it as ground.

- **The main valley** runs up the middle from the coast: up the farmland, along the chalet lake, past the village and its church, and up the narrow pass between the peaks. A turquoise river runs down it to the sea, with four little stone arch bridges. The escort route, the Squadron, the Valley Run and the flak follow it.
- **The railway** (27 km) climbs from a station by the town, through tunnels and over seven bridges, the stone viaduct 134 m over the main valley above the village, and up to the High Pass at 800 m. A steam train (an engine, its tender and three coaches) runs it back and forth. It is scenery only: no collider, so the battle never sees it.
- **Four dogfight arenas**, picked in the dogfight's briefing (or Random): **Peak Maze**, a bowl among the snowy peaks with canyons out of it; **High Pass**, the saddle the railway climbs to; **Chalet Lake**, up the lake past the village; **Town & Farmland**, open fields, the town and the coast. The arena only sets the start (`Turn/Core/Arena.cs`): the whole island is open once the fight begins. A start that would put a flight under 500 ft over the ground in its first turn and a half lifts that flight (only that one) clear.

The village is 26 copies of the Hi3D alpine chalet (`Art/Chalet`) round a cobbled square with a fountain, with a church (white nave, slate roof, stone tower with an open belfry and an eight-sided spire); 70 more chalets are farmhouses on the slopes and the farmland. The forest is dense: about 450,000 trees, a tree every 15 m or so wherever there is forest, filling the valley sides, the grass hills and the mountains' lower slopes up to the tree line (about 1,700 m, and below each mountain's snow and bare rock), all pines in the mountains and leafy woods and hedgerows on the farmland. They are not the terrain's own trees (too slow at this count) but `Scripts/Fx/Forest.cs`, which draws them in instanced 250 m squares: the full model near, a light mesh to about 1.2 km, and past that, out to 25 km, a pine is a plain cone without shadow. The clouds are banks of three to seven copies of the cloud model, most in a soft haze (`Art/Cloud/Haze.shader`). In the game they are drawn in the toon look of the smoke (`Scripts/Fx/ToonSky.cs`, put on every scene as it loads): each copy of the model is hidden and filled with round puffs facing the camera (`Fx/ToonCloud.shader`), cut flat along the base, cel-shaded white over periwinkle with a violet belly and inked in indigo round the outline of the mass. Each height has its own weather: flat grey-lavender banks low in the valleys, cumulus in the middle band (taller higher up), storm towers with an anvil, rain shafts and lightning in the upper band, and thin pink-shaded cirrus streaks above the peaks. Every bank drifts on the wind, swelling out of nothing and dissolving again as it goes, and its puffs billow; the haze shells are off. The sky is `Fx/ToonSky.shader`: a deep blue overhead through azure to a pale cyan horizon, a warm glow under the sun and a flat cartoon sun in two rings; the fog takes the horizon colour. The whole palette is at the top of `ToonSky.cs`.

The layout (`Editor/AlpineLayout.cs`, `AlpineMountains.cs`, `AlpineDress.cs`) is plain code with no Unity objects, so `Tools~/WorldPreview` runs it outside Unity (`dotnet run -- <dir>`): it checks the railway's grades, bridges and tunnels, the bombers' corridor, every dogfight start in each arena, and the dressing, and draws a map and a few views. Run **OODA > Build Test World**, then **Build Turn Battle**, to get the new island.

The source FBXs (50 to 100 MB each) aren't committed: near and far OBJs of each are made from them with Unity's mesh LOD generator the first time the world is built, if they are missing, and those are committed.

### Things to fly round, and the Valley Run

The valley has obstacles in the air as well as the rock: 10 to 16 grey barrage balloons 400 to 900 m over the basin and the main valley, each on a steel tether to a winch on the ground; two or three cable-car spans wall to wall across the narrow valley, two ropes with a car in the middle and a lattice pylon and station at each end; and four to six red and white lattice radio masts, 120 to 200 m tall, on the crests beside the valley, each with three guy wires. All of it is solid (the Obstacles layer): fly into a balloon, a tether, a rope or a guy and you crash, and rounds stop on them. The wires are thin to look at but 4 m thick to the battle. `AlpineWorld` places them from a fixed seed, off the bombers' route: nothing within 150 m of it unless it is at least 120 m under it (the cable cars hang about 135 to 165 m under the bombers). While planning, the wires within 3 km show as thin cyan lines, the balloons as rings, and each flak gun's reach as a faint red dome; the scene keeps their places in a `HazardMarkers` component. Run **OODA > Build Test World** again, then **Build Turn Battle**, to get them.

**Valley Run** (M in the battle cycles to it): you and a P-51 wingman low in the main valley, about 250 m over the floor among the ropes and the balloons' tethers, chasing two Bf 109s 1.8 km ahead up the valley, with the flak on the floor. Shoot both down to win. The AI pilots plan without seeing the obstacles, so they can fly into the wires too.

### Town Strike

**Town Strike** (M in the battle cycles to it) starts on a map of the island. Click the coast where the B-17s come in, then up to three turn points, each at Low (250 m over the ground beneath it), Medium (1,200 m) or High (2,400 m); 1, 2 and 3 set the selected point's height, Enter flies the plan. The bombers fly that path to the factory in the town in the middle of the island and on past it, climbing at 3 degrees and coming down at 6 at most. The map shades what the three radar sites can see at the selected point's height (the mountains cut holes in it), draws the patrol's loop round the town (not where on it they will be) and the 4 km ring inside which the town always sees you, and colours the path green where it is hidden, red where the radar sees it and black where it flies into the ground.

Until they are seen, 4 Bf 109s fly the loop and the other 4 wait on the runway. A radar site with a clear line to any of ours, the patrol within 4 km with a clear line, or any of ours within 4 km of the factory raises the alarm: the patrol attacks and the runway fighters take off a pair at a time, two turns apart, low and slow off its end. A bomber that passes over the factory drops on it; the mission ends when every bomber still flying has. The rules are `Turn/Core/Strike.cs`, the map and the alarm `Turn/TurnBattle.Strike.cs`, the town, runway and radar sites `Editor/AlpineTown.cs`. OODA > Strike Check flies it headless from three paths and logs what happens; `-strikeshots` on the built game's command line plans a path, saves the map and a turn as BMPs beside the exe, and quits.

The turn engine is plain C# in `Assets/OODA/Scripts/Turn/Core/`: the autopilot that flies the arrow (`AutoPilot.cs`), the reach ring (`Envelope.cs`), guns and hits (`Combat.cs`, `Battle.cs`), the AI (`EnemyAi.cs`) and the start (`Scenarios.cs`). Damage is to systems, not a health bar (below). The Spitfire IX's numbers and both aircraft's gun numbers are estimates from public figures, to tune by feel.

### Systems damage

There is no health bar. A hit knocks at one of four systems, and which one depends on where the round enters: the nose is the **engine**, the tail the **flight controls**, out along the wings the **wings**, and the fuselage between, from above or below, the **fuel** tanks. A round reaches the side it comes from, so the angle off decides: from dead astern you hit the tail, with a deflection shot the wings, head-on the engine. Each system takes `Battle.SystemShare` (0.6) of the aircraft's `toughness` (`CombatNumbers`; the wings `WingsShare`, 0.8) before it is knocked out, so about six cannon or .50 packets in one place; past half it is damaged and the aircraft flies worse:

| System | Damaged | Knocked out |
|---|---|---|
| Engine | power held to 60%, no WEP (no afterburner) | the engine stops: the pilot bails out under a parachute and the empty aircraft falls on and crashes |
| Fuel | leaking: the engine quits 30 s (ten turns) later (and the pilot bails out) | on fire: going down |
| Flight controls | half the roll rate, 30% less G | the tail is shot away: going down |
| Wings | 20% more drag, 25% less G | the wing folds: going down |

A missile's or a balloon mine's blast wears every system at once, by its damage over the toughness. The heat blade cuts the wings off. The reach ring, the ghost and the AI's tries all fly the damaged aircraft as it is. On screen each aircraft's row of four lights (ENG, FUEL, CTRL, WING) replaces the health bars: green, amber once damaged (a holed tank counts down its seconds), red once out; a damaged aircraft trails thin smoke. The rules are `Flight/Core/Systems.cs` (on each `FlightModel`, so a clone, a swap and a network snapshot carry it) and `Battle.Hit`. `Tools~/DamageCheck` (`dotnet run`) checks where rounds land, what each damaged system does to the flying, the leak, the blast, the bail-out, G-LOC, and that the damage survives a clone, a subset, a swap and a snapshot.

### G-LOC

Pull too hard for too long and the pilot blacks out. Past 6 G he builds up G stress (G-seconds over 6); at 4.5 of it he is out cold for 4 s: the stick and pedals go slack, the aircraft unloads to 1 G and flies on however it was banked, and nothing he planned happens until he comes round. Only once he unloads, under 3 G, does the stress drain, at 1 a second. So 8 G holds about 2 s past the onset, 7 G about 4 s, 6.5 G about 9 s: one full pull at speed greys you out but two in a row black you out, and slow, the wing can't pull the G to do it. While planning the warning line says when the turn would black you out (G-LOC); in playback the screen greys in from the edges as the stress climbs and goes dark with G-LOC across it while you are out. The AI pilots avoid it. The numbers are `GlocOnsetG`, `GlocLimit`, `GlocRecoverG`, `GlocRecoverPerSec` and `GlocSec` in `FlightModel` (free flight blacks out too).

### The menu and the replay

**Esc** (or the **Menu** button under Help) pauses the battle and opens the menu: Resume, Restart mission, Main menu (back to the title screen) and Quit game. When the fight is over the end screen has **Watch replay** (W), **Fly again** and **Main menu**.

**The replay** flies the whole fight again, turn after turn, with the tracers, the hits, the smoke and the pilots' gestures. Every turn is kept as it is flown, by aircraft in their starting order (`TurnBattle.Replay.cs`), so it follows each plane even after you Tab between yours. Any camera on any aircraft: **V** (or the View button) steps through chase, target, both and cockpit, **C** jumps in and out of the cockpit, **Tab** follows the next plane (Shift+Tab back, the pad's bumpers). **Space** pauses, the left and right arrows step a turn, up and down change the speed (a quarter to four times), the bar along the bottom jumps anywhere in the fight, and **Backspace** (pad B) leaves the replay. Drag or right-drag to look round, the wheel zooms.

### The briefing look

Every screen's text, the title, the planning panels, the buttons, the HUD and the end screens, is green monospaced type (Share Tech Mono) on dark olive panels with a thin green rim, and the big headings are stencil (Stardos Stencil). It all comes from one skin, `UI/BriefingStyle.cs`: each OnGUI calls `BriefingStyle.Use()` first, and any GUIStyle made from `GUI.skin` after that takes the look. The fonts are under `Resources/Fonts` with their SIL Open Font Licenses.

## Online play: a dogfight with a friend on another machine

**O** (or **Online** on the title) opens the online panel. One of you clicks **Host a game**: the panel lists this PC's addresses, Tailscale's 100.x first. The other types that address and clicks **Join** (port 7777 unless you add `:port`). Then the host picks **Co-op** (both of you on your flight against the AI, its planes shared out as evenly as they go, the host taking the extra one) or **Versus** (the host flies your flight, the friend the enemy flight, no easy aim), and **Pick the planes and fly** opens the dogfight briefing; its Fly starts the fight on both machines with the same setup (a random start is fixed by the host first). Over the internet, both install Tailscale and sign in, and the host shares the PC with the friend. Both need the same build: the hello refuses another one.

In the fight you plan your own planes (Tab goes round yours only). **Fly turn** sends your part and waits for your friend's; in co-op your wingman's ghost shows what they are planning as they plan it. A fight ends when a whole side is out, so you may watch your friend carry on after your planes go down. The host starts each new fight; OODA turns and switching missions are off online. If the connection drops, the friend's planes go back to their AI pilots and the fight carries on.

Under it, each turn only the plans cross the network: each player sends the plans, orders and formation shapes for its own planes, the host adds the AI's, flies the turn and sends every plane's plan with a hash of the battle after it; the friend flies the same turn and checks the hash, and takes the host's whole battle (a snapshot of about 1 KB) if they ever differ.

- `Turn/Core/Wire.cs` turns the battle, or any message, into bytes and back by walking their fields (so a new field is carried without listing it), and hashes the battle. Each machine keeps its own aircraft and gun numbers, hazard shapes and ground.
- `Turn/Core/Seating.cs` says who flies which plane and keeps the one order every machine agrees on: an aircraft's wire id is its index at the start, on the host. Each machine keeps its own plane at index 0 as Tab does, and `ResolveInWireOrder` flies the turn in wire order, because the order planes are stepped in decides the order rounds are fired and the gun dispersion's dice.
- `Net/NetLink.cs` is the TCP link (whole messages, a reader thread, a ping, lost after 20 s of silence); `Net/NetSession.cs` the session from the lobby into the battle, the messages, and `Lockstep`, how the host puts the two players' turns together. `UI/TitleScreen.Lobby.cs` is the panel, `Turn/TurnBattle.Net.cs` the fight.

`Tools~/NetCheck` checks it without the game (`dotnet run` there), including turns over real localhost sockets in co-op and versus. The built game tests itself too: `OODA.exe -nethost [-netsides 2 2] [-netversus] -netbot 10` and, in a second copy, `OODA.exe -netjoin 127.0.0.1 -netbot 10` fly ten turns by themselves and write every turn's hash to `OnlineLog-host.txt` and `OnlineLog-friend.txt` beside the dogfight log. **OODA > Build Windows Game** (or `-executeMethod Ooda.EditorTools.GameBuild.Windows -buildPath <exe>`) builds the game.

## Jet Test: the Phantom with an afterburner

A side test, kept apart from the WW2 missions: **Jet Test** in the bottom-right corner of the title (or **J**) puts you in an F-4 Phantom (the Hi3D model cut to about 50k triangles, `Art/Phantom`) in free flight over the Alps, 14,000 ft over the lake at the foot of the main valley at 420 kt. **Space** or pad **A** lights the afterburner (it pushes the throttle to the stop) and puts it out; pulling the throttle back with Ctrl or the left trigger puts it out too. The rest is the sandbox's controls, plus **Esc** back to the title. The HUD shows Mach big, the airspeed in knots and mph, and the best Mach of the flight; a flame and an orange glow come out of the nozzles when the burner is lit, and a vapour cone forms round the jet right at Mach 1 low down.

The numbers are `AircraftNumbers.Phantom()` (the asset `Settings/Phantom.asset`, remade by each build): jet thrust that hardly falls with speed and rises with ram, and a transonic drag rise (`ramPerMach2`, `waveRise` and the rest, zero for the propeller aircraft, which fly exactly as before). It tops out near 630 kt dry and 705 kt (Mach 1.07) in afterburner at sea level, and makes Mach 2 in about seven minutes of afterburner at 36,000 ft. Build it with **OODA > Build Jet Test** (after Build Test World and Build Title); it saves `Scenes/JetTest.unity` and adds it to the build.

## Cockpits, pilots and the art pass

**Cockpits.** The P-51, Spitfire and Bf 109 each have a cartoon cockpit in their FBX, under an empty called `Pit`: a painted tub with ribs, a padded leather coaming round a clean-cut opening, an instrument panel (airspeed, horizon, altimeter, climb, compass, RPM and small gauges), glare shield and gunsight, seat and armour plate, rudder pedals, a throttle quadrant with a trim wheel, a switch box, and the control stick (a spade grip in the Spitfire). Everything named `Pit_` uses one atlas, `Art/Cockpit/Cockpit_Atlas.png`. The stick, the throttle lever, the four needles and the horizon move.

**Pilots.** The tiger and the wolf share one rig and the same clips: **Fly** (the base loop: right paw on the stick, left on the throttle), **Instruments** (reaches to the panel, taps a gauge, twists a knob), **ShakeFist**, **CheckSix** (looks back over each shoulder), **Cheer** (fist pumps) and **Flinch**. `PilotControls` (on each pilot) keeps the paws on the stick and throttle with hand IK, leans the stick with the G and the roll, moves the throttle with the engine, drives the needles and the horizon, and plays the gestures: a fist at an enemy the pilot is watching once it is within 450 m, the instruments or a check of six now and then, and in the turn battle a flinch when hit, a fist when his rounds hit and a cheer for a kill. In the dogfight briefing each plane has a pilot button beside its aircraft: a click steps it through Usual (the type's own: the tiger in the P-51, the wolf in the others), Tiger, Wolf, Wizard, Skeleton and Bear. The F-4 keeps its crew. The bot takes `-dogfightpilots Wizard,Usual Skeleton` like `-dogfightcraft`.

**The bear.** A fifth pilot made the same way: the Hi3D bear in a winter flying jacket and a fur ushanka with a red star (`Art/BearPilot`), cut from 300k to 40k triangles with a 4K base map, rigged by `rig.py` (heat weights, the hat and ears pinned to the head) and given the six clips by `pilot_anims.py`. **The fox, the koi and the lion.** Three more on the same rig and clips, from Hi3D GLBs (`glb_prep.py ... feet` sets them on the ground): the fox and the koi in Japanese army tan with the rising sun on the sleeve (the fox in a leather helmet and goggles, the koi in a hachimaki headband), and the lion in an olive flying jacket with the Italian flag on the sleeve (`Art/FoxPilot`, `Art/KoiPilot`, `Art/LionPilot`), each cut to 40k triangles with a 4K base map. `rig.py` welds each GLB's seams before binding (heat weights for the fox and the koi; the lion's mane defeats heat weighting, so he is bound through a voxel-remeshed copy of himself and his mane pinned to his head). **The penguin.** One more on the same rig and clips: the Hi3D penguin in a leather flying helmet with brass goggles, a sheepskin-collared jacket and a white scarf (`Art/PenguinPilot`). `hi3d_prep.py` cuts him from 300k to 40k triangles, `hi3d_scale.py` makes him 1 m tall, and `hi3d_tex.py` makes the 4K base map and the 2K metal-smoothness map from Hi3D's separate metal and roughness maps. He is nearly all head and belly, so `rig.py` puts his shoulders at the roots of his flippers and his legs in the bottom of his belly over his feet (heat weights, the helmet and head pinned to Head); `pilot_anims.py` bakes the six clips. He seats in every fighter and is the last pick on the pilot button; the briefing's 4 v 3 test shot has him on the bear's wing.

**The spectre.** A chibi secret agent in a dark flight suit and harness whose head is a blue-black shadow with glowing cyan eyes, wreathed in ghost fire (`Art/SpectrePilot`). The Hi3D model is prepped like the bear (`hi3d_prep.py` to 40k triangles, `hi3d_scale.py`), rigged by `rig.py` (heat weights, the head pinned above the scarf) and given the six clips by `pilot_anims.py`; `spectre_tex.py` makes his maps: the 4K base colour with the head darkened (the eyes left bright), the metallic-smoothness map from Hi3D's separate metal and roughness maps, and an emission map lighting the eyes and the gloves. `PilotCast` gives his material that emission (HDR cyan) and puts `Pilot/SpectreFlames` on him: blue-white flames licking up round his head and from his hands and ankles, with a soft halo round each since the game has no bloom. They are billboards in one mesh rebuilt each frame on his bones and shaped and animated by `Fx/SpectreFlame.shader` (`Resources/SpectreFlame.mat`), so they follow every clip, the briefing's standing poses and his head look, and the head's flames go out with his head in the cockpit view; the eyes and gloves pulse with them. His face is always left clear. `SpectreFlames.look` picks his look: the aura (the usual one: the fire with a thin glowing mist close round his head and gloves), the fire alone, or a glowing fog of soft wisps that swell, churn, rise and fade off him; `-spectrestyle aura`, `flames` or `fog` on the game's command line sets it for every spectre. A small hot glow over each eye makes them blaze. He seats in every fighter and comes last on the pilot button; the briefing's 2 v 2 test shot has him flying a bandit Bf 109.

**The art pass.** Every texture was cleaned (edge-preserving smoothing that keeps the ink lines, seams filled so mipmaps don't pull in dark edges) and is now 4096 px; the P-51's and B-17's ragged invasion stripes are repainted straight from each texel's place on the wing; the cloud is repainted white with a soft grey-blue belly and its pock-marks smoothed; the flak gun is unwrapped and painted. The fighters' meshes lost the torn shards round the cockpit and the dents in their flat skin. The scripts that did it are in `Tools~/ArtPipeline` (Blender 5.2's `bpy` module, run headless); they were run from a scratch folder, so the paths at their tops need pointing at your copies of the `.blend` files. Export aircraft and props to Unity with `unity_export.py`, never Blender's FBX exporter on its own: a straight export leaves the root tilted and every empty pointing up, so the cockpit camera stares at the sky and the pilot is seated on his back. `nose_letters.py` repaints the P-51's MUSTANG bands from a side view of the nose.

**The polish pass.** The generated propellers were lumpy, so `prop_rebuild.py` replaces the P-51's, the Spitfire's and the Zero's with clean ones: a smooth ogive spinner with a rounded tip, and blades with airfoil thickness, twist, a rounded tip and a crisp yellow tip band. Their UVs point into a small flat-colour palette that `palette.py` paints into an unused corner of each plane's atlas, so every plane keeps its single material. On the P-51, with its canopy hidden, you could see past the cockpit rim into the tub, and torn canopy-rail shards stuck up round it. `sill_fairing.py` cuts the skin beside the opening along a clean line and lofts a side panel from the rim down to the skin, with an inked seam. On every fighter it also closes the open front of the rear deck behind the seat, and `build_cockpit.py` now builds that bulkhead itself. `p51_markings.py` redraws the P-51's markings as exact shapes from the position map (`bakepos.py`): straight invasion stripes on the wings, tailplane and rear fuselage, the star-and-bar insignia, the LH / I codes and the 472216 tail number reading the right way on both sides. It also lightens the fin's heavy outline, and `thin_fin.py` slims the fin itself. `wing_panels.py` straightens the panel lines and rivet rows on every fighter's wings and tailplane. The generated lines were straight within each texture piece but jogged where the pieces met on the model, so it unrolls each surface into a flat span-by-chord chart, finds the ink there, refits each stroke as straight pieces and each rivet row as evenly spaced dots, and draws them back from each texel's true position so they line up across the seams. It also redraws the Bf 109's wing crosses, the Spitfire's roundels and the Zero's hinomaru as exact shapes. On the P-51 it runs before `p51_markings.py`. Each script's header gives its arguments, and the specs used are in `Tools~/ArtPipeline/specs`.

## First run

1. Install **Unity 6** (any 6000.x) with Unity Hub.
2. Clone this repo outside OneDrive (OneDrive locks Unity's Library folder), for example `C:\dev\ooda-dogfight`.
3. In Unity Hub, choose **Add > Add project from disk** and pick the folder. If the Hub asks which version, pick your Unity 6.
4. If Unity asks to enable the new Input System backends and restart, say **Yes**.
5. In the menu bar choose **OODA > Build Test World**. It sets up URP, the models' import settings, materials, the pilot's animator, the `P51` prefab and the `TestWorld` scene.
6. Press **Play**.

## Tuning the flight

The P-51D's numbers are in `Assets/OODA/Settings/P51D.asset` (made by Build Test World). Select it and change them while flying. They are seeded from public figures (wing area, drag, Merlin power, G limits, stall speed, roll rate) and are estimates; `AircraftNumbers.cs` lists the sources.

| Number | What it does |
|---|---|
| `maxG`, `minG` | The G limits the stick asks for at full deflection |
| `stallKias` | 1 G stall speed: the most G the wing gives is (KIAS / stallKias)² |
| `rollPerKtasDps`, `maxRollRateDps` | Full-stick roll rate: grows with true airspeed up to the cap |
| `gOnsetGps` | How fast G builds |
| `thrustK`, `dragA`, `dragB` | Thrust and drag (the OODA tool's fit shape) |
| `wepFactor` | Extra thrust with war emergency power |

## Checking the port

The C# core flies the same paths as the OODA tool, to the printed digit, for level flight, a loop and two banked turns:

```
cd Tools~/FlightCoreCheck
dotnet run -- <path to dads-debreif>/src/core
```

It also prints both aircraft's top speeds, climb, turn and roll numbers, then checks the turn engine: the reach ring, the autopilot arriving on the arrow, rounds hitting and dropping, and two AI pilots fighting from the head-on start.
